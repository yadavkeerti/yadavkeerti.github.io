Every summer, Indians are advised to avoid the afternoon sun, drink enough water and reduce intense outdoor activity during periods of extreme summer heat. For the Agariyas of Gujarat’s Little Rann of Kutch, that advice exposes a fundamental limitation of India’s approach to heat.

Their workplace is the heat. For months each year, Agariya families migrate into the Little Rann, where they live in temporary settlements and produce salt by pumping underground brine into pans and allowing the sun to evaporate the water. Recent reporting from the region describes how Agariya families can spend up to eight months in the salt pans, often facing severe constraints in access to water, food and healthcare.

The same climatic conditions that make salt production possible also create an exceptionally demanding occupational environment. India has made important progress in recognising extreme heat as a climate and public-health challenge. Heat Action Plans have expanded across the country, while Gujarat’s State Action Plan on Climate Change and Human Health identifies vulnerable groups including outdoor workers and calls for measures such as access to potable water, shaded areas and cooling spaces. Yet the Agariyas reveal a more specific policy problem.

Climate policy recognises heat. Labour policy increasingly recognises workers. Public-health systems recognise heat-related illness. Climate adaptation increasingly promotes technological solutions. These systems, however, do not always converge where some of India’s most climate-exposed workers live and work and that is where the heat-policy blind spot of India exists.

When Heat Is the Workplace

Most policies are based on a straightforward assumption: when temperatures become dangerous, people should reduce their exposure. That assumption does not work for everyone.

For the Agariyas, exposure to heat is inseparable from livelihood. Salt production depends on evaporation, and evaporation depends on the climatic conditions in which workers spend long hours carrying out physically demanding labour. Heat is therefore not simply an occasional disaster. It is part of the production system. Evidence from salt-pan workers elsewhere in India demonstrates why this distinction matters. A 2023 peer-reviewed study of 352 salt-pan workers in Tamil Nadu found that 87% exceeded recommended occupational heat-stress thresholds. Researchers documented frequent dehydration-related symptoms and found an association between high occupational heat stress and reduced kidney function. The study concluded that workplace interventions to prevent heat stress and dehydrationwere urgently required. These findings should not be automatically transferred to the Agariyas without location-specific health research. They nevertheless underline a larger concern: salt-pan work in India can involve a combination of intense heat exposure, physical labour, dehydration and inadequate opportunities for recovery. This matters because India’s public conversation on extreme heat still focuses largely on immediate emergencies such as heatstroke, hospital admissions and mortality. For workers exposed to high temperatures over extended periods, the consequences can also be cumulative. Repeated dehydration and physiological heat strain may create risks that are less visible than a heatwave emergency but remain serious occupational-health concerns. For the Agariyas, therefore, climate change cannot be understood only as an environmental problem. It must also be understood as a question of occupational health.

The Double Climate Trap

Heat is only one part of the Agariyas’ climate vulnerability. Salt production depends on seasonal rhythms and the Little Rann’s environmental conditions. Recent reporting has documented how extreme conditions that support salt farming can simultaneously place considerable strain on workers, while disruptions to seasonal patterns can threaten production and livelihoods. This creates what may be described as a double climate trap. Extreme heat threatens workers’health and climate instability threatens their livelihoods. This distinction is important for India’s adaptation policy.The ability to adapt to heat is not determined by temperature alone. A worker may understand perfectly well that dangerous temperatures require rest and reduced exposure, yet remain unable to stop working if doing so means losing the income needed to survive. Climate vulnerability is therefore also economic vulnerability. This is particularly relevant for informal and seasonal workers. Advice to avoid outdoor activity during the hottest hours can be difficult to follow when livelihood depends directly on continued exposure. ORF has previously highlighted a related weakness in India’s Heat Action Plans: broad definitions of vulnerability can fail to capture how occupation, social conditions and the ability to avoid exposure shape climate risk. The Agariyas provide a particularly clear case of this problem.

Beyond the Category of ‘Outdoor Workers’

India’s heat policies increasingly recognise outdoor workers as a vulnerable population. This is an important step.Broad categories, however, can conceal important differences. A construction worker in a city, an agricultural labourer in a village and a salt worker living seasonally inside the Little Rann may all be classified as outdoor workers. Their exposure, living conditions and ability to access protection can nevertheless be fundamentally different.

The Agariyas face a distinctive combination of risks: prolonged exposure to heat, physically demanding work, seasonal migration, geographic isolation and constraints in accessing essential services. Vulnerability must therefore be understood in greater detail. Climate risk is shaped not only by exposure to a hazard but by the conditions that influence people’s sensitivity and capacity to respond. The Agariya’s workplace exposes them directly to extreme environmental conditions. Their ability to reduce that exposure may be constrained by the economic and seasonal structure of salt production. Their access to healthcare and other essential services can be affected by the geographic isolation of the salt pans. A generic category such as ‘outdoor workers’ may therefore be too blunt an instrument for designing effective protection. The challenge for India’s next generation of Heat Action Plans is not simply to identifymore vulnerable groups. It is to understand how and where vulnerability is produced.

A Solar Pump Cannot Make a Salt Pan a Safe Workplace

The Little Rann also raises an important question about how India understands climate adaptation. Solar-powered pumps have transformed aspects of salt production in Gujarat. Recent research by the International Center for Research on Women and SEWA has examined the experiences of Agariya women salt-pan workers as they transition from diesel to solar pumps. The research explores the relationship between clean technology, economic resilience, gender and climate adaptation. A more recent policy brief based on mixed-methods research conducted between 2023 and 2025 similarly examines how solar technology is reshaping women’s work, wellbeing and resilience while highlighting inequalities in access, ownership and benefits. These developments are important. They should not, however, lead to an overly simple conclusion that technological change automatically produces climate resilience.

A solar pump can make salt production cleaner.

It can reduce dependence on diesel.

It can improve aspects of production and livelihoods.

It cannot, by itself, make a salt pan a safe workplace.

It cannot provide shade during dangerous heat.

It cannot ensure reliable drinking water.

It cannot guarantee healthcare access in remote seasonal settlements.

And it cannot remove the economic consequences of weather disruptions.

Technology can improve adaptation without eliminating vulnerability.

This distinction matters as climate policy increasingly measures progress through technologies deployed, renewable energy generated and infrastructure created. The more fundamental question is whether people’s exposure to climate risk has actually been reduced. Recent work on informal women workers makes a similar argument: climate resilience requires more than clean technology. It also depends on secure livelihoods, essential services, resources and agency.For the Agariyas, genuine resilience must mean more than cleaner energy. It must mean the ability to earn a livelihood without sacrificing health.

Where India’s Policy Systems Fail to Meet

The Agariyas sit at the intersection of four policy systems. Climate policy addresses rising temperatures and changing environmental conditions. Labour policy addresses workplace conditions and worker protection. Public-health policy addresses heat-related illness and healthcare access. Climate adaptation policy increasingly promotes technologies intended to strengthen resilience.

Each system addresses part of the problem. The Agariyas demonstrate what can happen when these systems do not fully converge. A climate shock can affect salt production and reduce income. Reduced income can constrain a household’s ability to respond to health risks. Heat exposure can undermine health. Poor health can reduce the ability to work. These risks reinforce one another. Policy responses should therefore be better integrated. India does not lack climate programmes, heat policies or health interventions. The larger challenge is fragmentation. Heat warnings, occupational protection, healthcare access and adaptation technologies are often developed through separate institutional systems. For labours these issues are inseparable.

From Heat Action Plans to Heat-Safe Work

India’s expanding heat-governance architecture provides an opportunity to address this gap.

The next generation of Heat Action Plans should move beyond warning systems and emergency response towards a stronger framework for occupational heat protection.

Four priorities deserve attention. First, India needs occupation-specific heat-risk assessments. Heat Action Plans should identify not only geographical hotspots but occupations facing particularly intense exposure. Salt-pan workers, agricultural labourers, construction workers and other outdoor workers experience different combinations of environmental and economic risk. Second, occupational heat should be integrated into climate-health surveillance. Monitoring heatstroke remainsessential. Repeated dehydration and other longer-term physiological consequences of occupational heat also require greater attention. Climate-health systems should be capable of identifying chronic risks rather than responding only to emergencies. Third, heat-safe workplaces should be treated as climate-adaptation infrastructure. Reliable drinking water, shaded rest areas, work-rest schedules, adjustments to working hours and accessible emergency healthcare should not be treated as optional welfare measures. In a warming climate, they are essential forms of adaptation. Finally, climate adaptation must account for the economic cost of avoiding heat. A worker cannot follow advice to stop working during dangerous temperatures if doing so means losing the income required to survive. Social protection and livelihood security must therefore become part of India’s response to occupational heat.

A Test for India’s Climate Future

The Little Rann of Kutch may appear remote from India’s major policy centres. The challenge unfolding there is not.As India becomes hotter, millions of workers may increasingly face the same dilemma: they may know that extreme heat is dangerous yet remain unable to avoid it because their livelihoods depend on working through it. The Agariyasoffer an early warning of this future. India has made important progress in forecasting heat and responding to heat-related emergencies. It is investing in technologies that can support climate adaptation. The next challenge is more difficult. India must learn how to make work safe in a hotter world. For the Agariyas, escaping the heat has never been a realistic option. The people who make a product found in virtually every Indian kitchen should therefore become a test case for India’s climate-adaptation strategy. A heat warning can tell people when danger is coming. A solar pump can make production cleaner. Climate resilience, however, will remain incomplete until workers can earn a livelihood without sacrificing their health. The question is no longer whether India recognises heat as a crisis. The question is whether India’s climate policy is ready to protect the people who must continue working through it.
